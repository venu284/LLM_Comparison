## 2. Per-source notes

### 2.1 Route Anything (arXiv 2611.05275; code and data)

**What it claims.** A theory of routing as "value minus priced cost given what the router observes"
(Theorem 2, p. 4–5), an "information ladder" from the best fixed candidate to the per-input oracle
(Proposition 3, p. 5), and a certified quality floor (Proposition 5, p. 5–6). It measures these on
a proprietary omni-modal matrix (§3–§4) and on two public priced testbeds, CodeRouterBench and
RouterBench (§5, Appendix C).

**Findings that bear on this project.**
- *Cheap direct dispatch is already strong on CodeRouterBench.* §5.1, Finding 6 (p. 11): "On
  CodeRouterBench the router fitted for zero quality loss never calls Opus … It dispatches every
  task to a cheaper model and reaches 25.5× Opus's efficiency at a 0.44-point loss (Table 5)."
  Table 10 (p. 27): 21 cells at ε = 0 cost 0.452 m$ per task against 11.655 for Always-Opus, with
  ΔV −0.0044 [−0.0175, +0.0097]. "Best fixed cascade 2.004 [1.881, 2.140]". The states "need not be
  oracle labels: prompt-inferred cells reach 25.3×" (p. 11). A fitted floor is not a guarantee: it
  "holds on test in only 12.5% of 200 splits", and certification restores it (p. 11).
- *Within-type signal is small on CodeRouterBench.* Finding 1 (p. 7): "The best released
  CodeRouterBench routers capture 55% of the value of perfect routing information when the task
  type is unknown, but only 6–8% within a task type (Appendix C.5)." Table 15 (p. 30): DimensionBest
  native capture −1.468; the LLM forecast reaches "test AUROC 0.541 and 0.618 and a capture of
  −0.037 [−0.225, 0.077]" (C.5, p. 30).
- *Value-of-information bound.* Proposition 9 (A.8, p. 22): a pre-attempt signal "is therefore worth
  buying only if k < a(1 − s) ≤ a: it avoids at most one unsuccessful attempt per task, whatever its
  accuracy."
- *Post-failure planning (C2).* Proposition 8 (A.8, pp. 21–22) and Finding 7 (p. 12), quoted in the
  table. In X10, plans that use post-failure rates are shorter than marginal-planned ones (mean
  length 1.54 against 2.08) and realise a higher J on the test split in 27 cases against 2 where
  they differ (X10 README, claim C4).
- *Verifiers (C3).* Table 14 (p. 30): compiler gate AUROC 0.766, visible examples 0.537, hidden
  tests 0.994, LLM judge 0.824. "For Qwen3-Max at θ = 0.5, a free compiler gate gives 4.09× dollar
  efficiency at a 2.4-point loss, and an LLM judge 2.63× if free but 0.30× at its list price"
  (Finding 7, p. 12). Sandbox cost: "a hidden-test sandbox run 0.15 s and $5.2 × 10⁻⁶ (2.0%)" of a
  Qwen3-Max call, priced at AWS Lambda x86, 2 GB, us-east-1, September 2026 (C.4, p. 30).
- *Memory (C1).* Appendix C.6 (p. 31): a semi-synthetic store over "2,044 probing and 867 test
  tasks in 12 cells", writing on verifier acceptance with exploration probability 0.25. Its prior is
  "always-Opus or the probing-fitted DimensionBest". With hidden-test writes it scores 0.116 below
  DimensionBest.
- *Compare on the same inputs.* §1, key finding 5 (p. 3), detailed in §4.4 (p. 10): a fixed baseline that drops a model for a few
  missing scores turned a "0.4-point deficit into a 2.4-point gain".

**The key question: are the 15% / 66.5% figures already published?**
- *Paper (arXiv v1).* **No.** It gives no split by visible-test presence. It reports only the
  visible-examples AUROC 0.537 on the X8 sample (Table 14, p. 30) and the hidden-test TPR/FPR
  0.960/0.205 (C.6, p. 31).
- *Repository.* **Not the same numbers, but equivalent ones.**
  - X13's verifier table gives W1 FPR by source on the 867 test tasks: all sources 0.539; MBPP
    0.995, DS-1000 0.960, CRUXEval 0.515, HumanEval 0.429, LCB 0.145, HE 0.111, BCB 0.107. The
    sources with no visible tests are the high-FPR ones. X13 does not split by `w1_n` (checked in
    `README.md`, `run.py` and `results_claims.json`).
  - X7b claim C5 does split explicitly, on 283 live tasks. At θ = 0.5, the share of a model's
    attempts that are false accepts is 0.012–0.056 on prompts with examples and 0.304–0.373 on
    prompts without. The chain-level quality loss is −0.092 to −0.141. The visible chains reach
    η/η_B of about 0.9–2.3×, against 3.9–10.6× for hidden-test chains.
  - X8's V1 curve shows the same split by threshold. For Qwen3-Max on the test sample, FPR is 0.716
    at θ = 0.5 (no-example tasks accepted) and 0.009 at θ ≥ 0.55 (examples required and all
    passed).
- *Population differences.* Our 15% / 66.5% come from the X13 verdicts over 2,911 tasks from both
  splits, all eight models, binary-truth cells. X13's table covers 867 test tasks, X7b covers 283
  EXEC-CLEAN test tasks, and X8 covers 334 (an EXEC-CLEAN subset restricted to tasks whose
  libraries import). Our 15% with visible tests is much higher than X8's 0.009 at θ ≥ 0.55. The
  populations and verifier definitions differ, and the cause of the difference was not
  established here. (X13's per-source FPRs mix tasks with and without visible tests, so they
  cannot be compared with X8's 0.009 directly.)
- *Our 66.5% is not a visible-test figure.* It is the X13 syntax/quality heuristic on cells with
  no visible tests, which S0.11 does not use. S0.11 treats those cells as no signal.

**What C3 still adds.** The web-dev E4 measurement: a live visible-versus-held-out split with a
controlled visible fraction f, on a different domain (multi-file web apps with Jest and Playwright
tests), and the false-pass curve over f. On CodeRouterBench, it adds the economics of a learned
no-signal decision at headline scale, which is more a C2 result than a C3 one.

**C2: does Route Anything already predict "stop or escalate", "no model will solve it", or
truncate early?**
- *Stop or escalate:* yes, as a rule (Prop. 8), with cell-level primitives and an oracle verifier
  (X10).
- *Early truncation:* yes, at the cell level (X10 C4).
- *"No model will solve it":* no, except descriptively. The terminal step always calls the
  reference, and the give-up variant appears only in X10 C5 (OOD, n = 176, "INCONCLUSIVE").
- *Per-task prediction:* only before the first attempt (X12a/b and `paid_forecast`), where it fails
  to capture value within a cell (C.5).

**Data facts found while checking.**
- `verdicts.jsonl` has 23,288 rows over 2,911 tasks.
  - **Empty responses.** 1,551 rows have `empty = true`: glm-5 554, qwen3.5-plus 538, MiniMax-M2.7
    379, kimi-k2.5 79, gpt-5.4 1. In all 1,551, `w1_accept` and `w2_pass` are false and `w1_n` is
    0. 877 of them have a released truth ≥ 1. The file does **not** impute the released truth, so
    replaying it does not leak the answer.
  - **Where the empty rows land.** All of them have `w1_n = 0`, so they fall in the no-visible-test
    bucket. They cannot affect the 15% / 27% figures for cells with visible tests. They do affect
    the no-signal share (79.4% of cells) and the heuristic 66.5% / 17.6% figures.
  - **The 611 tasks.** Of the 611 tasks whose visible-test count differs between models (S0.11),
    **all 611 differ only because of empty rows**: after the empty rows are dropped, no task has
    differing `w1_n`. The per-cell variation in S0.11 is therefore a missing-response artefact, not
    a property of the tasks (counted with a scratch script outside the repo, on the pinned
    revision).
  - X13's own analysis imputes the released truth on empty cells (X13 README, Setup: "every
    verifier is imputed the released truth on them"), so its derived tables should not be reused
    for replay.
- The HF card says: "Missing recorded runs include 148 zero-score/zero-cost Qwen3-Max rows; zero
  recorded cost is not a claim that a real call would be free." These are missing runs scored as
  failures, not real failures.

### 2.2 ORACLE (arXiv 2607.22465), including Appendix E.8

**What it claims.** A training-free feedback loop on top of any model selector for concurrent
agentic programs, with three parts:
- *adaptive verification:* a task-type classifier picks the verifier;
- *delayed feedback:* verification runs off the critical path;
- *DISC:* a KV-cache-aware dispatch scheduler.

It is evaluated on SWE-bench Verified, τ²-bench and Terminal-Bench 2.0, with a two-model pool
(Qwen3.5-9B, Qwen3.6-27B). The abstract reports "up to 7 percentage points over state-of-the-art
routing baselines" at matched cost.

**Appendix E.8 (p. 24), quoted in full.** "Fig. 4 reports the router's steady state after eight
passes over the stream. Replayed cold, in a single pass with family and domain context only,
ORACLE coincides with random mixing of the two models: +0.9 pp [−1.4, +4.3] at the midpoint cost
on the τ²+SWE+TB 2.0 stream, +1.6 [−1.3, +5.7] on τ²+SWE and −0.8 [−2.1, +2.6] on SWE+TB 2.0. The
frontier gain therefore emerges with accumulated task-level feedback; a single cold pass over the
stream is insufficient to reach the steady-state improvement reported in Fig. 4."

**The review's claim is confirmed.** All three cold-start intervals include zero. Two related
passages:
- B.6 (p. 17): "Blocks whose reading was not met are reported as such, for example the cold-start
  frontier (Appendix E.8)". The cold-start reading was pre-registered and was not met.
- B.4 (p. 17): "the steady state (Fig. 4) runs eight passes over the stream and scores the last,
  the cold start (Appendix E.8) one pass."
- E.6 (p. 23) explains part of the steady-state gain for the ACRouter selector: "The memory starts
  empty for each seed and persists across passes, so from the second pass a repeated task finds
  its own earlier verdict among its neighbors; this recall is what lifts the orchestrator's
  accuracy at its released cost setting above the 27B alone." The same section on ACRouter's
  released memory rule: "The memory rule needs many verdicts per dimension before its means
  separate, which these streams do not supply."

**What this means for C1.**
1. Published in-stream learning from verified outcomes, with no prior and a fixed two-model pool,
   gives **no measurable gain in a single cold pass**. The gain ORACLE reports needs repeated
   passes, and at least for the ACRouter selector some of it comes from recalling the same task's
   earlier verdict. C1 is therefore not covered by ORACLE: ORACLE has no new-model arrival, no
   pooled prior, no stopping rule and no k-matched lookup comparison.
2. ORACLE E.8 is the strongest published motivation for C1's design: a pooled prior and a stopping
   rule are what in-stream learning lacked in one pass. It also raises the bar. E1 must be scored
   on a **single pass with no repeated tasks**, or C1 inherits ORACLE's steady-state artefact.
3. C1's framing should not promise that in-stream learning beats probes. ORACLE and Route
   Anything C.6 are two published cases where in-stream verified feedback did not beat a static
   baseline.

**Overlap with C3: small.** ORACLE's verifiers are the benchmarks' own graders (B.5: "Verdicts come
from the SWE-bench harness … its state checker as the verdict … container tests"). It studies
verifier **mismatch**: a patch verifier gives no information on τ²-bench (§3.1, p. 3; E.3, p. 21).
It never measures false-pass against a held-out grader. The only verifier error it models is
misclassification: "a task meets the wrong verifier with probability 1 − q for its family's
measured q and is then credited 0" (B.3). §8 of the plan lists ORACLE as related work for C3; it
fits C1 (online feedback) better.

### 2.3 The Routing Plateau (arXiv 2606.07587)

**What it claims.** Across 21 routers and five benchmarks (RouterBench, Nine-by-30k, EmbedLLM,
R2-Bench, CARROT), "many methods, including kNN, achieve very similar accuracy and converge to a
narrow performance range that remains far below the oracle router" (abstract). §4.2 (p. 4): "the
top-5 methods differ by only 0.22 pp on average", and "the best configurations remain 10.6–29.4 pp
below the oracle router." The proposed cause is a "correctness-prediction bottleneck". Ten times
more data, larger encoders and fine-tuning add "1.24 pp on average" (abstract). §6.4 (p. 9): "further
progress may require enriching the inputs to routing with evidence from model execution, such as
partial output trajectories or confidence estimates derived from an initial attempt."

**Noise check.** §4.3 (p. 5) and B.5 (pp. 24–25, Table 22): ten-sample majority labels on 5,000
Nine-by-30k test queries give an oracle of 0.817 against 0.827, "retaining about 93% of the gap".

**Overlap with C4: partial.** It shows that oracle headroom mostly survives label noise on its
benchmarks. None of the five benchmarks is CodeRouterBench, and it does no artefact or grader
audit. It does not contradict C4; it gives C4 a method (multi-sample labels) and a reference
result. Its §6.4 also supports C2's direction: using execution evidence after a failed attempt
rather than query features alone.

### 2.4 Most of the LLM Routing Gap Is Task Type (arXiv 2608.23023)

**Identity.** Confirmed: arXiv 2608.23023 v2 is this paper (Janghoon Lee, Redrob). The id in §8
of the plan is correct.

**What it claims.** On 14 models × 294 questions (7 task types × 3 languages; 14 items per cell),
run twice at temperature 0:
- "5.37% of the 4,116 model-question pairs came out scored differently anyway" (abstract);
- under the both-run rule, "Assigning each task type one model in advance, chosen once and never
  updated, improves 21 of the 29" items that routing could improve, and adding language "leaves
  6 of 294 unoptimized" (abstract);
- the residual is concentrated in coding: "Of the 8 items left inside a task type under the
  both-run rule, 7 are coding" (§1, p. 2).

The paper is explicit about its limits: "All of this is fitted and scored on the same 294
questions with no holdout" (abstract); §4.3 (p. 7–8): "That is not a claim that a learned router
is useful."

**Overlap with C4: partial.** It measures run-to-run noise and compares the routing residual with
it, which is the logic of C4's noise-corrected oracle. Its matrix is small, in-sample and not code
execution at scale, and it does no artefact audit.

**Does it undercut DimensionBest as the main baseline? No.** It supports it: a static per-type
table captures most of the gap (21 of 29 items in-sample). It does contradict Route Anything's
omni-modal finding that "92% within types" (Figure 3, p. 7) of the gap is unreachable by type. On
CodeRouterBench, however, Route Anything's own C.5 result (55% capture without type knowledge,
6–8% within type) agrees that dimension identity carries most of the capturable value. The
stronger static yardstick to add next to DimensionBest is Route Anything's 21-cell lookup
("CellBest", C.2, p. 28) and its cost-aware zero-loss route (Table 10), not something weaker.

## 7. IRT-Router (arXiv 2506.01048), read for P0.5

Date: 2026-11-19. Source: arXiv [2506.01048](https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01048) **v2, 21 Jun
2025**, full text from the PDF (§1–§7, Limitations, Appendices A–C). Identity checked against the
PDF header: "IRT-Router: Effective and Interpretable Multi-LLM Routing via Item Response Theory",
Wei Song, Zhenya Huang, Cheng Cheng, Weibo Gao, Bihan Xu, Guanhao Zhao, Fei Wang, Runze Wu (USTC,
NUS, NetEase Fuxi). Code: `github.com/Mercidaiha/IRT-Router` (not opened). Page numbers are the
PDF's.

### 7.1 Answers to the P0.5 questions

- **How does it represent a new LLM?** By an embedding of a text profile, not by outcomes. §4.1
  (p. 5): "each LLM Mj (e.g., GPT-4o) is represented by a corresponding embedding eMj ∈ RdM, which
  is derived from its profile. The profile includes metadata such as the model's release date,
  developer, type, key features, and a brief description (see Table 10)." The ability vector is a
  learned linear map of that embedding, §4.2.1 (p. 5): "θMj = Wθ eMj, ai = Wa eqi, bi = Wb eqi,
  (4) where Wθ, Wa and Wb are all learnable weights." The profiles were written with a search
  engine, Appendix A.1 (p. 13): "we obtained each LLM's profile by utilizing ChatGPT's web search
  mode with prompts. Of course, the final results were manually corrected." Table 10 (p. 16) lists
  the 20 training profiles and one for the new model.
- **Does it need outcomes of the new model, and how many?** None. Training uses the outcomes of
  the 20 known models only: §5 (p. 6), "the training set is defined as Dtrain = {(qi, Mj, yij), qi ∈
  Qtrain, Mj ∈ M, yij ∈ [0, 1]}", with M the 20 candidates of Table 5. The new model, Claude 3.5
  Haiku, enters only through its profile, §6.3 (p. 9): "We also conduct experiments on the new LLM,
  Claude 3.5 Haiku 20241022. Here, we focus on evaluating how different routers perform in
  predicting the quality of the new LLM's responses on the ID test set." This is k = 0 onboarding.
- **How well does that work?** Weakly, by the authors' own account. Table 4 (p. 9), new-LLM
  prediction at α = 0.8: RouterBench AUC 0.50, ACC 0.34; MIRT-Router AUC 0.62, ACC 0.67;
  NIRT-Router AUC 0.62, ACC 0.68. §6.3 (p. 9): "the current IRT-Router still shows limited
  generalization to unseen LLMs, with an ACC of 0.67, indicating significant room for improvement.
  We believe improving this aspect is also an important future direction, such as leveraging
  few-shot learning or similarity to warm up LLM cold-start." The new model is scored on prediction
  error only (MAE, RMSE, AUC, ACC), not on routing quality or cost.
- **In-stream learning?** No. The router is trained offline and fixed. The Limitations section
  (p. 9) names continual training as future work: "allowing IRT-Router to evolve and train
  continually, we expect its accuracy to improve further." Its "warm-up" is for **queries**, not
  models, §4.4 (p. 6): "we update its vector as eqi = (1 − λ) · eqi + λ · e^warm_qi, (10) where
  e^warm_qi is an adjustment vector obtained by averaging the embeddings of its k-nearest neighbors
  in the training set".
- **Verifier?** No. Labels are ground-truth scores against reference answers, §4.2.1 (p. 5):
  "yij is the empirical performance score of Mj on qi. This score is computed by comparing the
  LLM's response to the ground truth." There is no run-time verifier and no escalation.
- **Stopping rule?** No. The router routes once per query to the highest-scoring model, §4.3
  (p. 5): "The LLM with the highest score is the one to which the query is routed." Nothing decides
  when to stop collecting evidence about a model.
- **Comparison against a lookup table built from the same outcomes?** No. The baselines are
  Small LLM, Large LLM, HybridLLM, RouteLLM and RouterBench's predictive router (§5.3, p. 6). The
  new-LLM test (Table 4) compares against RouterBench only, which also gets no outcomes of the new
  model. No per-group or per-dataset lookup table is used.
